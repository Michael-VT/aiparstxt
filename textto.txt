LLM models don't actually understand BGP—they only attempt to describe it.

This is precisely what is currently slowing down the adoption of AI in network security and acceleration, and leading to confusion in human understanding after AI implementation.
If you ask a general-purpose model to diagnose a BGP problem, you'll get a confident, well-structured answer that's often incorrect. The model correctly explains the concepts, but fails when those concepts interact in a real network.

The problem isn't intelligence, but context. BGP is unique in that it's complex because:
• State over time—past events directly influence current behavior
• Topology dependence—the same route appears different from different perspectives
• Policy management—every network behaves differently
• Failure ambiguity—problems are often partial, not binary

LLM models don't see your routing tables, topology, or policies. They match patterns in documentation, not reason about your network.

That's why their answers seem correct, but lead nowhere—or, worse, suggest changes that could disrupt the system.

For AI to be truly useful in networks, you need more than just hints:
• Current network state
• Topology awareness
• Temporal memory
• Policy context
• Safe, consequence-aware actions

The real gap isn't knowledge, but operational understanding.

AI won't replace network engineers until it can think in context, not just talk in theory.
However, current events suggest that in the near future, all of this will become quite simple and transparent.
